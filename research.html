Research

research

We connect the processes that generate structural variation with the evolutionary forces that shape it.

01

How often do genomic rearrangements arise?

Genomic rearrangements include duplications, deletions, inversions and more complex changes in genome structure. Their rates of occurrence differ across genomic regions, shaping the variation we observe in populations.

We aim to measure how often genomic rearrangements arise in the human germline using deep long-read sequencing and connect these estimates with population genomic data.

Long-read sequencing · Germline rearrangements · Population genomics
02

Why do some genomic regions rearrange repeatedly?

Repeated stretches of DNA provide substrates for recombination. Their number, length, orientation, and sequence similarity can influence which rearrangements form. Gene conversion can alter those substrates further.

We investigate how local genomic architecture, meiotic recombination, and interlocus gene conversion interact to sustain structural variation hotspots.

Genome architecture · Recombination · Gene conversion
03

How do recurrent rearrangements shape evolutionary history?

Similar genomic rearrangements can arise independently within populations and across species. Accounting for these recurrent events is essential when reconstructing evolutionary histories or assessing evidence of selection.

We combine comparative genomics, ancestry graphs, and population-genetic models to investigate the origins and consequences of structural variation, including variants inherited through archaic introgression.

Comparative genomics · Population-genetic models · Archaic introgression

Where the questions began

Lessons from the amylase locus.

Clio’s work with collaborators reconstructed the structurally complex human amylase locus and identified independent rearrangements across primates. These studies motivate broader questions about recurrent genomic rearrangements, genomic context, and evolutionary convergence.
